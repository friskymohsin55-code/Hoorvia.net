Position Sizing Chart: Printable Trading Risk & Size Guide

Most traders blow up accounts not by picking the wrong stock, but by betting too much on the right idea. A trade with a good setup and a bad size still ruins the week when the stop-loss hits — because the size, not the idea, decides how much money leaves the account. Position sizing fixes that with one rule: risk only one to two percent of your account on any single trade, then let the stop-loss distance decide how many shares that buys. A tight stop means more shares; a wide stop means fewer; the dollars at risk stay identical. This printable chart lists the twelve position sizing rules and formulas traders actually use, from the shares formula and risk-reward ratios to the Kelly criterion and volatility sizing. 💡 Quick Tips

Risk first, size second: Decide the dollars at risk before you look at the share price — size follows risk.

Respect the 2% ceiling: No single trade should ever risk more than two percent of the account.

Recalculate every trade: Your account size changes after every win and loss, so the risk dollars change too.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I calculate position size for a trade?

Divide the dollars you are willing to risk by the distance from your entry to your stop-loss. Risking $100 with a $2 stop distance buys 50 shares. The formula is (account × risk%) ÷ (entry − stop).

What is the 1% rule in trading?

Risk no more than one percent of your account on a single trade. On a $10,000 account that is $100 per trade, so ten consecutive losses still leave $9,000 — the rule exists so a bad week never ends the account.

What is a good risk-reward ratio?

Two-to-one or better: risk $1 to make $2. At 2:1 you can be wrong half the time and still break even. Ratios below 1:1 demand a very high win rate and quietly drain accounts over time.
