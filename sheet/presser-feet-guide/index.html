Presser Feet Guide: Printable Sewing Machine Feet Chart

A sewing machine ships with one foot on it, and most people never take it off — then wonder why zippers pucker, quilts shift, and vinyl sticks and skips. The presser foot is the part that holds the fabric against the feed dogs, and each foot is shaped for one job: the zipper foot hugs the teeth, the walking foot feeds thick layers evenly, the Teflon foot glides over sticky vinyl, and the blind hem foot hides the stitch entirely. Using the wrong foot does not just look worse — it fights you, bunching fabric and breaking needles. Twelve feet cover nearly every sewing job, and most of them cost less than a spool of good thread. This printable chart lists the twelve presser feet every sewer should know, with what each does, the job it suits, and the tip that makes it work. Consult the live reference table below, or print the sheet and keep it by the machine.

💡 Quick Tips

Match the foot to the job: A zipper foot for zippers, a walking foot for quilts — the right foot does half the work.

Snap them on and off: Most modern feet snap on in seconds, so swapping mid-project costs nothing.

Test on a scrap first: One test seam on scrap fabric catches skipped stitches before they ruin the garment.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a presser foot on a sewing machine?

The presser foot is the flat piece that presses fabric against the feed dogs as you sew. It holds the fabric steady, sets the seam allowance, and different feet add special functions — zippers, buttonholes, hems, and gathering all need their own foot.

Which presser foot should I use for zippers?

The zipper foot. It is narrow on one side so the needle can stitch right beside the zipper teeth, and it works for both invisible and regular zippers as well as piping and welting. Move the needle left or right to hug the teeth.

Do I really need a walking foot for quilting?

Yes for anything thick or slippery. A walking foot feeds the top and bottom layers together, so quilt sandwiches, plaids, and vinyl stop shifting and puckering. Without one, the top layer creeps ahead and the seams never match.
