Multiplication Grid 1–12 & 1–20: Printable Times Table Chart

Multiplication facts are the bottleneck of all later math: if 7×8 still takes ten seconds of finger-counting, long division, fractions, and algebra all grind to a halt. Guessing from partial memory is worse than not knowing, because a wrong fact practiced a hundred times becomes a habit. What kids actually need is a single reliable reference they can check in seconds — one that builds the pattern sense of skip-counting, squares, and the 12s and 20s they meet in measurements and money. This printable chart gives a quick-lookup multiplication grid for the numbers 1–12 with ×5, ×10, ×12, ×20, and square values in each row. Consult the live reference table below during homework, or print the sheet below for the desk or fridge — a checked fact today becomes an automatic fact next month.

How to Use This Chart

Pin this chart where you work: it packs 12 entries and 6 columns (Number, × 5, × 10, × 12, × 20, Square (n²)) into one printable page, built for quick lookups.

Scan down the Number column to your item, then read across for × 5, × 10, × 12, × 20, Square (n²).

Example: for 1, the chart gives × 5 of 5.

  • Compare values down a single column to spot the pattern before you commit to a choice.
  • When two rows look close, the last column usually breaks the tie.
  • Print it once and keep it where you work — that's the whole point of a cheat sheet.

💡 Quick Tips

Learn the Squares First: The twelve squares (1, 4, 9, 16, 25 … 144) anchor the grid — nearby facts sit one row away.

Use ×5 as a Bridge: Every multiple of five ends in 0 or 5, so 7×6 is 7×5 plus 7 — find the nearest five and adjust.

Skip-Count Aloud: Count 12, 24, 36 aloud while pointing at each row — hearing the rhythm beats silent reading.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the fastest way to memorize multiplication facts?

Practice a few facts at a time with mixed recall — flashcards, timed quizzes, or skip-counting games — instead of reading the table top to bottom. Start with easy rows (×2, ×5, ×10), then squares, then the tricky 7×8. Short daily drills on a printed chart beat one long session.

Why do schools teach multiplication up to 12?

Twelve is the biggest highly composite small number — it divides evenly by 1, 2, 3, 4, and 6, which makes fractions, dozens, inches, and clock math natural. The 12×12 table captures these patterns, while the ×20 column covers money, measurements, and the twenties kids meet in real life.

Should kids learn multiplication by memorizing or understanding?

Both — understanding comes first through groups and arrays, then memorization makes the facts automatic. A child who knows 6×4 as ‘six groups of four’ and has also drilled it recalls the answer instantly. Use the chart to check answers while learning, then print it for timed practice.
