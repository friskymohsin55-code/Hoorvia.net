Car Mileage Maintenance Schedule Chart: Printable Service Intervals Guide

Your car's owner's manual hides the single most valuable page in the whole book: the maintenance schedule. Skip it and you get the classic surprises — an oil change that should have cost forty dollars becomes a seized engine that costs four thousand, a ten-minute tire rotation becomes two bald tires and a tow. Most drivers do not know whether their oil is due at 5,000 or 7,500 miles, when spark plugs are actually due, or that brake fluid quietly absorbs moisture until the pedal feels spongy. This printable mileage maintenance schedule lays out the standard manufacturer intervals for the services that matter: oil, filters, tires, brakes, coolant, transmission fluid, spark plugs, belts, and the battery — each with the mileage window, what the shop actually does, and why it matters. Consult the live reference table below before you book your next service, or print the sheet below and keep it in the glove box.

💡 Quick Tips

Follow Your Manual First: These are standard ranges — your owner's manual always wins for your exact car.

Time Also Counts: Some services age out by time (3 years), not mileage — low-mileage cars still need them.

Log Every Service: Write the date and mileage after each job so the next interval is always obvious.

Frequently Asked Questions

How often should I really change my oil?

For most modern cars on full synthetic oil, every 5,000–7,500 miles is the standard range. Older conventional-oil schedules of 3,000 miles are outdated for newer engines. Your owner's manual lists the exact interval for your car — trust it over any shop's sticker.

What maintenance is most commonly skipped?

Transmission fluid service and brake fluid flushes are the two most skipped jobs, because nothing feels wrong until damage is done. Both are inexpensive compared to the transmission or brake repairs they prevent — follow the chart intervals.

Do low-mileage cars need less maintenance?

Less mileage-based maintenance, yes — but not zero. Fluids degrade by time, not just miles: oil, brake fluid, and coolant still need replacing every few years. Batteries also age out. Keep the time-based rows on the chart regardless of your odometer.
