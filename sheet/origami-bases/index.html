Origami Bases Chart: Printable Classic Fold Foundations

Every classic origami model — the crane, the jumping frog, the waterbomb balloon — begins with the same small library of starting shapes called bases. Beginners waste hours following full models without realizing the first six steps are always a base they could have learned once and reused forever. Learn the dozen traditional bases and the instructions of almost any traditional model suddenly read as familiar moves: precrease, collapse, petal fold, done. Each base also teaches a folding technique — squash folds, rabbit ears, blintz folds — that transfers directly to modern designs. This printable chart presents 12 classic bases, from the simple kite base to the blintzed bird base, with how each is folded, the shape it makes, and the famous models it starts. 💡 Quick Tips

Repetition builds hands: Fold each base until you can do it without looking — then every traditional model gets dramatically easier.

Practice on copy paper: Cheap 70–90 gsm copy paper creases crisply and folds well. Save the expensive washi for finished models.

Creases decide everything: Sharp, accurate precreases make a base collapse cleanly. Bone-fold every crease before collapsing.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the easiest origami base to learn first?

The kite base — fold two adjacent edges to the diagonal center line and you are done. It is the foundation for swans and simple birds, and it teaches the edge-to-center move used in several harder bases.

Why do the bird base and frog base look alike?

Both are built by petal-folding: the bird base petal-folds a preliminary (square) base, while the frog base petal-folds a waterbomb base. The technique is identical; the starting base is what changes the flap count.

What is a blintzed base?

Any base folded after blintz-folding the paper first — folding all four corners to the center. The classic example is the blintzed bird base, which has far more flaps than a plain bird base and was used by Akira Yoshizawa for his famous crab.
