Seafood Cooking Time & Temperature Chart: Printable Fish & Shellfish Guide

Seafood goes from perfect to ruined in under a minute, which is why even experienced cooks second-guess shrimp timing and salmon doneness. Because different species vary wildly in thickness, fat content, and structure, a single rule never fits all — delicate scallops need a hard sear while dense lobster tails need gentle oven heat. This printable seafood cooking time and temperature chart covers twelve popular fish and shellfish with exact cook times, oven temperatures, and target internal temperatures, plus visual doneness cues for shellfish where thermometers cannot reach. Check the table below before your fish hits the heat, or print the sheet below to keep by the stove for weeknight dinners and dinner parties alike.

💡 Quick Tips

The 10-Minute Rule: Measure fish at its thickest point and cook about 10 minutes per inch of thickness at 400–450°F. This classic rule works for nearly every finfish fillet and steak.

Carryover Matters: Fish keeps cooking off the heat. Pull salmon and halibut 5°F below your target temperature and rest 3 minutes — the center will coast up while staying silky.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the safe internal temperature for fish?

The FDA recommends 145°F for finfish, measured at the thickest part. Many chefs prefer salmon at 125–130°F for a silky medium-rare texture, which is a quality choice rather than a safety rule for high-quality, fresh fish.

How do I know shrimp are done without a thermometer?

Watch the shape and color. Raw shrimp are gray and straight; perfectly cooked shrimp turn pink with white flesh and curl into a loose C shape. A tight O shape means they are overcooked and will be rubbery.

Should I discard mussels or clams that do not open?

Yes. Shellfish that stay tightly closed after full cooking time were likely dead before cooking and may be unsafe. Discard them. Also discard any with cracked shells or a foul smell before cooking.
