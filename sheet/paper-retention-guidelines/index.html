Paper Retention Guidelines: Printable Document Storage Chart

Every drawer of old paperwork asks the same question — keep it or shred it — and the wrong answer costs either a missing document at audit time or a filing cabinet full of dead paper. The IRS gives clear windows: most tax records need three years from filing, bad-debt and worthless-security claims need seven, employment tax records need at least four, and filed tax returns themselves are worth keeping forever. But tax is only half the story: pay stubs, bank statements, mortgage papers, and medical bills each have their own clock, and irreplaceable originals like wills and deeds never get shredded at all. This printable chart lists twelve document types with exactly how long to keep each, why that period matters, and what to do when it ends. 💡 Quick Tips

Three years is the baseline: Most tax records are safe to shred three years after filing — only bad-debt claims stretch the clock.

Shred, don't toss: Old statements carry account numbers, so cross-cut shred anything with personal details.

Scan the keepers: Scan forever-documents and store copies in two places; the original stays in a fireproof safe.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long should I keep my tax returns?

Keep the filed return itself forever — it is your proof of filing. Keep the supporting documents, like W-2s and receipts, for three years from the filing date, or seven years if the return claims a bad-debt or worthless-security loss.

What happens if I shred something too early?

If the IRS audits a year whose records you shredded, you lose the proof behind your deductions and may owe tax plus penalties. When in doubt, keep it one more year — storage is cheap, but a missing receipt in an audit is not.

Should I keep paper originals or are scans enough?

The IRS accepts scanned copies if they are complete and readable, so scans work for most tax records. Keep paper originals only for irreplaceable documents — wills, deeds, birth certificates, and titles — stored in a fireproof safe.
