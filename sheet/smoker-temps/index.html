Smoker Temperature Chart: Printable Low-and-Slow Smoking Reference

Great barbecue is 90 percent temperature control and 10 percent everything else. Because smoking works by holding meat in gentle heat for hours while smoke and rendering fat do their slow magic, guessing either the smoker temperature or the finish temperature is how briskets stall into shoe leather and ribs turn to mush. This printable smoker temperature chart covers twelve barbecue staples with the exact smoker temperature range, target internal temperature, and realistic cook time for each — from a 16-hour packer brisket to weeknight chicken wings. Check the table below while your smoker comes up to temp, or print the sheet below to keep by the pit for competition-worthy results.

💡 Quick Tips

Respect the Stall: Brisket and pork shoulder famously stall around 150–165°F for hours as moisture evaporates. Do not raise the heat — wrap in butcher paper (the Texas crutch) and wait it out.

Rest Like You Cooked: Rest large cuts 1 hour wrapped in a cooler; rest ribs 15–20 minutes tented. Slicing a brisket straight off the smoker spills a cup of juice you spent 14 hours building.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the best smoker temperature for brisket?

225–250°F is the sweet spot. Lower (225°F) gives more smoke flavor and a darker bark but takes longer; 250°F powers through the stall faster with slightly less smoke penetration. Either way, cook to 200–205°F internal, not to a clock.

Should I wrap my ribs, and when?

Wrapping (the 3-2-1 method: 3 hours smoke, 2 wrapped, 1 sauced) speeds cooking and tenderizes, but softens the bark. For competition-style bite-through bark, skip the wrap and spritz with apple juice every 45 minutes instead.

Why did my smoked chicken skin turn rubbery?

Chicken skin renders and crisps above 300°F, but most smoking happens at 225–275°F. Smoke the bird to 150°F internal, then finish over direct high heat or in a 425°F oven to crisp the skin while the inside coasts to 165°F.
